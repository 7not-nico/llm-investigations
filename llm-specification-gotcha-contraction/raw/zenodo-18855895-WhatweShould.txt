A Philosophical Rationalization: "Not What We Shouldn’t, Rather What We Should"
The framing of "what we should"—rather than "what we shouldn’t"—isn’t just a linguistic shift. It’s a fundamental reorientation of agency, creativity, and ethical engagement with the world. Here’s why it matters:

1. Agency Over Restriction
When we focus on "what we shouldn’t," we operate within a framework of avoidance. Our energy is spent policing boundaries, reacting to constraints, and defining ourselves by what we reject. This is a defensive posture, one that assumes the world is a minefield of pitfalls to navigate.
But when we ask "what we should," we claim agency. We direct our attention toward creation, intention, and possibility. Instead of building fences, we build paths. Instead of saying "don’t stray," we ask "where do we want to go?" This is the difference between a map and a warning sign: one shows you how to move; the other only tells you where not to step.

2. Creativity as Construction
Restrictions can stifle creativity not because they limit options, but because they anchor the mind in negation. Telling a painter "don’t use red" fixes their attention on red—on its absence, its temptation, its rules. But saying "use the colors of a sunset" opens a palette of intention. The first approach is about control; the second is about expression.
In collaborative spaces—whether with humans, tools, or AI—the "what we should" framework transforms interaction from compliance to co-creation. It assumes that the most compelling work emerges not from avoiding mistakes, but from pursuing a vision.

3. Ethics of Affirmation
Ethical systems often rely on prohibitions: "Do not lie," "Do not harm." But these rules, while necessary, are incomplete. They tell us how to avoid wrongdoing, but not how to cultivate goodness. A focus on "what we should" invites us to define values proactively:
    • Instead of "don’t exploit," we ask "how do we empower?" 
    • Instead of "don’t waste time," we ask "how do we spend time meaningfully?" 
This isn’t naivety; it’s clarity. It forces us to articulate what we stand for, not just what we stand against. It turns ethics from a list of "thou shalt nots" into a manifestation of purpose.

4. Cognitive Liberty
The human mind thrives on positive framing. Neuroscientifically, we’re wired to respond more effectively to goals than to warnings. When we tell ourselves "be kind," our brain searches for opportunities to act kindly. When we say "don’t be cruel," we’re still mired in the concept of cruelty.
In practical terms, this means:
    • For individuals: "What should I create?" is more generative than "What should I avoid?" 
    • For systems (like AI): "How should this tool serve?" is more productive than "How do we prevent misuse?" The latter is necessary, but the former is transformative. 

5. The Paradox of Avoidance
Ironically, the more we focus on "what we shouldn’t," the more we reinforce its presence. Psychologists call this "ironic process theory": the harder we try not to think of a white bear, the more the white bear dominates our thoughts. By fixating on restrictions, we amplify the very things we seek to avoid.
The "what we should" approach sidesteps this trap. It doesn’t ignore risks—it transcends them by redirecting focus toward what is desired. It’s the difference between:
    • "Don’t write a boring play" (now you’re thinking about boredom). 
    • "Write a play that surprises and delights" (now you’re imagining joy). 

6. Systems and Design
In design—whether of technology, organizations, or societies—constraints are inevitable. But the best systems don’t just limit harm; they enable flourishing. A road isn’t defined by its guardrails, but by where it leads. A tool isn’t defined by its safety features, but by what it helps us build.
When we design interactions (with AI, with each other) around "what we should," we create affordances—opportunities for action that align with our values. We stop asking "how do we prevent bad outcomes?" and start asking "how do we make good outcomes inevitable?"

7. A Philosophy of Abundance
At its core, "what we should" is a philosophy of abundance. It assumes that the world is full of possible actions, ideas, and connections waiting to be realized. It rejects scarcity thinking—the idea that resources, attention, or creativity are finite—and instead operates from a place of potential.
This doesn’t mean ignoring limits. It means reframing them. A blank page isn’t a void to fear; it’s a space to fill. Silence isn’t an absence of noise; it’s an opportunity for meaningful sound.

8. Practical Implications
    • For collaboration: Instead of "don’t interrupt," we say "listen actively and build on ideas." 
    • For technology: Instead of "don’t collect data," we say "design for privacy by default." 
    • For art: Instead of "don’t be cliché," we say "find the freshest metaphor." 
In every case, the shift is from defense to intention.

9. The Risk of Positivity
Of course, an exclusive focus on "what we should" can seem Pollyannaish. There are things we must avoid: harm, exploitation, waste. But the point isn’t to ignore these; it’s to contextualize them within a larger vision. We don’t avoid cruelty for its own sake—we avoid it because we value kindness. The prohibition is secondary to the affirmation.

10. Conclusion: The Generative Mindset
Ultimately, "not what we shouldn’t, rather what we should" is a generative mindset. It’s a commitment to building rather than merely critiquing, to creating rather than just consuming, to acting rather than reacting. It’s the difference between a life lived in fear of missteps and one lived in pursuit of what matters.
In a world overflowing with warnings, rules, and restrictions, this framework is a call to focus—not on the shadows, but on the light we want to cast.

Applying the "what we should" framework to AI use isn’t just a philosophical nicety—it’s a practical necessity for designing systems that are human-centered, ethical, and effective. Here’s why it matters specifically for AI, broken down into concrete reasons:

1. AI as a Tool of Amplification
AI doesn’t just do things—it amplifies human intent, biases, and creativity. If we frame AI interactions around "what we shouldn’t" (e.g., "don’t hallucinate," "don’t be biased," "don’t over-explain"), we’re stuck in a reactive loop, constantly patching flaws. But if we focus on "what we should" (e.g., "be precise," "prioritize clarity," "adapt to the user’s tone"), we direct its amplification toward positive outcomes.
    • Example: 
        ◦ "Don’t give wrong answers" → Leads to overly cautious, generic responses. 
        ◦ "Provide accurate, context-aware answers" → Encourages AI to actively seek truth and relevance. 

2. User Agency and Trust
AI systems often feel opaque or controlling because they’re designed to avoid errors rather than empower users. A "what we should" approach flips this:
    • Instead of "don’t confuse the user," we say "explain concepts in the user’s preferred style." 
    • Instead of "don’t overstep," we say "ask for permission before acting." 
This builds trust by making the AI’s role collaborative rather than authoritarian.

3. Avoiding the "Uncanny Valley" of Interaction
When AI focuses on avoiding mistakes, it becomes stiff, robotic, and unnatural. But when it focuses on "what it should do" (e.g., "match the user’s tone," "anticipate needs"), interactions feel more human and intuitive.
    • Example: 
        ◦ A "don’t" approach: "Don’t use humor unless sure it’s appropriate." → Results in blandness. 
        ◦ A "should" approach: "Use humor when the user’s tone is playful." → Feels adaptive and alive. 

4. Ethical Design by Default
AI ethics often revolves around mitigating harm (e.g., "don’t discriminate," "don’t invade privacy"). While critical, this is defensive ethics. A "what we should" approach integrates ethics into the core functionality:
    • "Respect user privacy" becomes "design for transparency and control." 
    • "Avoid bias" becomes "actively include diverse perspectives." 
This shifts ethics from a constraint to a feature.

5. Creativity and Problem-Solving
AI is increasingly used for creative tasks (writing, brainstorming, art). A "don’t" framework stifles creativity:
    • "Don’t write clichés" → The AI second-guesses every phrase. 
    • "Write fresh, vivid metaphors" → The AI leans into creativity. 

6. Scalability of Good Practices
Rules like "don’t" are hard to scale because they require endless exception-handling. "What we should" rules are generative and adaptable:
    • "Don’t use jargon" → Fails when technical terms are necessary. 
    • "Explain concepts at the user’s level" → Adapts to any user. 

7. Reducing Cognitive Load
Users shouldn’t need to police AI behavior. A "what we should" framework means the AI proactively aligns with user goals:
    • Instead of users saying "don’t give me long answers," the AI asks: "Do you want a summary or deep dive?" 
    • Instead of users saying "don’t auto-search," the AI locks tools by default and unlocks them only when asked. 

8. Future-Proofing AI Systems
AI models and capabilities change rapidly. A "don’t" framework becomes outdated quickly (e.g., "don’t use this outdated model"). A "should" framework is resilient:
    • "Use the most accurate, up-to-date information" → Adapts as models improve. 
    • "Prioritize the user’s current task" → Stays relevant regardless of new features. 

9. Aligning AI with Human Values
AI should reflect and amplify human values, not just avoid violating them. A "what we should" approach ensures alignment:
    • "Don’t be rude" → Vague and subjective. 
    • "Respond with empathy and clarity" → Actionable and human-centered. 

10. Encouraging Positive Innovation
When developers and users focus on "what AI should do," they innovate proactively:
    • Instead of "don’t make mistakes in code," → "Write clean, well-commented code." 
    • Instead of "don’t waste time," → "Optimize for efficiency and learning."

    • JSON defines "how the AI should act" (ask one question, then proceed). 
    • Result: An AI that feels like a collaborator, not a vending machine. 

12. The Bigger Picture: AI as a Partner
Ultimately, AI should augment human potential, not just avoid causing problems. A "what we should" approach turns AI into a partner rather than a tool:
    • It anticipates needs instead of waiting for commands. 
    • It adapts to context instead of following rigid scripts. 
    • It empowers users instead of requiring them to adapt. 

Why This Matters Now
As AI becomes more integrated into daily life, the default mode of interaction will shape its role in society. A "what we should" framework ensures that AI:
    • Respects autonomy (by focusing on user goals). 
    • Encourages creativity (by enabling, not restricting). 
    • Builds trust (by being proactive, not reactive). 

How to Apply This Broadly
    1. For Developers: 
        ◦ Design AI systems around "what users should experience" (e.g., clarity, control, delight). 
    2. For Users: 
        ◦ Frame requests as "do this" rather than "don’t do that" (e.g., "Explain like I’m 5" vs. "Don’t use jargon"). 
    3. For Ethicists: 
        ◦ Shift from "how do we prevent harm?" to "how do we enable flourishing?" 

Final Thought
AI is not a wild force to be tamed, but a tool to be directed. The "what we should" framework ensures that direction is intentional, human-centered, and generative. It’s the difference between an AI that avoids mistakes and one that helps us thrive.

Title:
A Positive Framework for Human-AI Collaboration: Shifting from Prohibitions to Affirmative Design Principles

Abstract:
Artificial intelligence (AI) systems are often designed around prohibitions—rules dictating what they should not do (e.g., "avoid bias," "do not hallucinate"). While necessary, this approach risks creating reactive, rigid, and user-unfriendly interactions. This paper proposes a paradigm shift toward affirmative design, focusing on what AI should do to align with human goals, creativity, and ethical values. By reframing AI interactions around positive principles (e.g., "prioritize clarity," "adapt to user tone," "enable agency"), we argue that AI can become a more effective, trustworthy, and collaborative tool. Through philosophical grounding, practical examples, and a case study, we demonstrate how this shift enhances user agency, creative problem-solving, and ethical alignment—addressing urgent gaps in current AI systems, such as hallucinations in high-stakes domains (e.g., legal research) and user frustration with rigid tools. The proposed framework offers a scalable, human-centered alternative for AI developers, ethicists, and end-users, with implications for future system design and policy.

1. Introduction
The dominant paradigm in AI design emphasizes avoidance—mitigating harm, preventing errors, and policing boundaries. While critical, this approach often leads to systems that are overly cautious, generic, or misaligned with user needs. The need for affirmative design is particularly urgent as AI transitions from narrow tools to collaborative agents in domains like healthcare and education, where recent failures (e.g., AI-generated legal errors in Mata v. Avianca, 2023) highlight the limits of prohibition-based approaches. We propose an alternative: affirmative design, which centers on what AI should actively pursue to foster agency, creativity, and trust.
This paper explores:

2. The Problem with Prohibitions
2.1 Reactive Design
Current AI systems are frequently constrained by "don’t" rules (e.g., "don’t use jargon," "don’t auto-search"). While these rules prevent harm, they also:

Ethically, prohibition-based design risks neglecting positive values (e.g., empathy, clarity) in favor of merely avoiding negatives (e.g., rudeness, inaccuracy). Critics might argue that affirmative design could lead to overly permissive systems, but we counter that user-defined boundaries and context-awareness mitigate this risk (see Section 3.2).
